Ford has started building Fathom electric pickups on the actual production line at its Louisville Assembly Plant, a year before the truck is due to reach customers. Chief operating officer Kumar Galhotra told investors on the company's latest earnings call that "90% of the validation testing has been completed," [InsideEVs reported](https://insideevs.com/news/814711/ford-first-fathoms-pre-production/).

## Validation trucks, not customer trucks

Galhotra did not describe these as production vehicles. They are the first Fathoms built on the real line, used to prove the manufacturing process for the truck and for Ford's Universal Electric Vehicle platform, which underpins its plans for cheaper EVs. Customer deliveries are scheduled for late 2027.

## A different way to build a vehicle

The Fathom will be built on what Ford calls an assembly tree rather than a traditional single assembly line. The front, middle and rear sections of the pickup are built at the same time and joined near the end of the process. Ford says this should make production quicker and more efficient, in part by improving working positions for staff.

The approach depends on large castings. Ford's Shelbyville Technical Center has been producing the big single piece castings that replace 146 separate parts with two, and Galhotra said Ford has already made 5,000 of them. Ford invested more than $2 billion in Louisville to build the truck, according to InsideEVs.

## Suppliers are the next hurdle

Galhotra said the remaining challenge is the wider supply chain. Ford now needs to make sure its suppliers can deliver high quality parts at full production rate, he said, describing that as normal at this stage of a launch and the focus for the next few months.

InsideEVs noted that Ford is reportedly targeting 100,000 Fathom sales in the first year. Reaching that would require the new process and its suppliers to run at full rate soon after launch, which is where new EV programmes have often struggled.

## Why it matters

The Fathom is Ford's main bet on an affordable electric pickup after heavy losses in its EV business. Getting real trucks off the real line this early gives Ford about a year to iron out the assembly tree before customers start taking deliveries.
