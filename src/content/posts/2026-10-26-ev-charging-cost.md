The U.S. Energy Information Administration's [Electricity Monthly Update](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/update/print-version.php) lists the August 2026 residential retail price at 18.16 cents per kilowatt-hour, 3.4 percent higher than August 2025. The page's release date is 23 October 2026, and the next release date it prints is 24 November 2026. EIA says it does not directly collect retail electricity rates. It calculates average retail revenue per kilowatt-hour from sales revenues and volumes, and it uses that average as a proxy for price.

The same table lists commercial revenue at 14.47 cents per kilowatt-hour in August 2026, up 4.3 percent from a year earlier, and the all-sector average at 14.87 cents, up 5.1 percent. A home bill follows the residential line. On state averages for that month, the update says California, Massachusetts, and Rhode Island had the highest average revenues in the contiguous United States, at 29.02, 26.42, and 25.34 cents per kilowatt-hour. It says North Dakota, Louisiana, and New Mexico had the lowest, at 8.91, 9.86, and 10.08 cents per kilowatt-hour. A household pays its own tariff. The 18.16 cent figure is a national proxy for August 2026.

[FuelEconomy.gov's charging page](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/charging.shtml) answers a narrower question with a worked example, and the example does not use 18.16 cents. It says that if you drive an electric vehicle that gets 97 MPGe, which it also writes as 35 kilowatt-hours per 100 miles, 12,000 miles a year, electricity costs $0.13 per kilowatt-hour, and you only charge at home, charging would add $45.50 to the monthly electric bill. This desk recorded that page on 27 October 2026. It does not show a publication day. Those inputs are 4,200 kilowatt-hours a year. At $0.13 per kilowatt-hour, 4,200 kilowatt-hours is $546 a year, and $546 divided by 12 is $45.50, the monthly figure the page prints. The page does not call $0.13 the current U.S. average. Put EIA's 18.16 cent August average into the same 4,200 kilowatt-hours and the year comes to about $762.72, or about $63.56 a month. That calculation is this desk's, not either agency's, and a household's own tariff decides the real figure. How MPGe itself is defined is in this desk's [explainer on the U.S. EV label](/articles/2026-10-29-epa-ev-label-range/).

The [Alternative Fuels Data Center home-charging page](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-charging-home) gives the arithmetic without a monthly bill. It says the cost per mile needs the electricity price and how many kilowatt-hours the vehicle uses to travel 100 miles. Its example: if electricity costs 10.7 cents per kilowatt-hour and the vehicle uses 27 kilowatt-hours to travel 100 miles, the cost per mile is about $0.03. A second example on the same page: at 10.7 cents per kilowatt-hour, charging an electric vehicle with a 200-mile range, assuming a fully depleted 54 kilowatt-hour battery, costs about $6 to reach a full charge. The page says the actual residential cost depends on the equipment, the time of day, and how long the station is used, and that some utilities offer residential time-of-use rates. This desk recorded the page on 27 October 2026. It does not show a publication day. The 10.7 cent price is the page's example.

Cold weather changes the kilowatt-hours per 100 miles those formulas use. AAA's 2026 laboratory test, covered in this desk's [cold-weather range explainer](/articles/2026-10-25-ev-range-cold-weather/), found a lower calculated range at 20°F than at 75°F for the three electric vehicles in that test. More energy per mile raises the electricity those examples multiply. The FuelEconomy.gov and data-center examples are not winter figures.

Where the car charges changes the price and the speed. The data center's [charging-stations page](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-stations) says most electric-vehicle owners do the majority of their charging at home. The typical miles of range each charging level adds, from both agencies, are in the section on charging speed below.

The home-charging page says many owners can meet daily driving range requirements by charging overnight with the Level 1 cordset that comes with the vehicle, with no additional cost or installation, if an outlet on a dedicated branch circuit is available near where they park. It says Level 2 equipment can be installed for drivers with less regular schedules, longer commutes, or large batteries that need more than an overnight charge. Outdoor equipment has to be outdoor-rated. Charging at multifamily housing, it says, needs extra consideration and may be more like public charging than charging at a single-family home. This desk's story on the [LAZ and EverCharge joint venture](/articles/2026-10-23-laz-evercharge-apartment-charging/) is about that kind of building. The announcement does not name an installation price.

FuelEconomy.gov separates the charger from the electricity. It says home charger units generally cost between about $400 and $1,000. It says the installation cost depends on the electrician, the complexity, and any permits or other fees, and that incentives may offset some of the cost. It does not print a labor price. It says a plug-in hybrid with a small pack, or a driver who covers a limited number of miles on electricity each day, may be served by Level 1, and that a large pack and 50 or more electric miles a day may make a Level 2 charger worth the money. That is the page's guidance for a driver deciding on equipment. It is a range for the unit, and it leaves the installation quote to the house.

Public charging is a different price. FuelEconomy.gov says some public chargers are free and others use a flat fee, a monthly subscription, a charge for the electricity, or a charge for time, and that rates vary by region and network. It says there are over 81,000 public charging stations in the country. That station count is a different unit from a count of DC fast-charging ports. This desk's story on [ChargePoint's U.S. DC fast ports](/articles/2026-10-24-chargepoint-us-dc-fast-ports/) uses another dataset. A posted price in another country is also separate from the U.S. residential average. [Tesla's four open Singapore bays](/articles/2026-10-26-tesla-singapore-chargers-open/) are priced per kilowatt-hour in Singapore cents, with a higher band for non-Tesla cars, on The Straits Times' account.

## Charging at home in the UK

Great Britain's price cap is a different number from the U.S. average. [Ofgem's announcement](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/news/changes-energy-price-cap-between-1-october-and-31-december-2026) for 1 October to 31 December 2026, published on 26 August 2026, says a household on a standard variable (default) tariff paying by Direct Debit will pay on average 26.32 pence per kilowatt-hour for electricity, with a daily standing charge of 54.83 pence. Ofgem says that is the average across England, Scotland and Wales, and that VAT has been removed from electricity bills from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. It says households that have moved to a fixed-rate tariff are not affected by the cap change. The regulator lists 25 November 2026 as the date it will announce the level for 1 January to 31 March 2027.

Ofgem's [unit rates page](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/information-consumers/energy-advice-households/energy-price-cap-unit-rates-and-standing-charges) prints the previous quarter beside it: 26.11 pence per kilowatt-hour and a 57.19 pence standing charge from 1 July to 30 September 2026. It says costs cannot be compared directly with earlier periods because of the VAT change, and that unit rates and standing charges differ by region and by payment method. On multi-rate tariffs such as Economy 7, it says, a household pays one rate at peak times and another off-peak, and together they cannot be more than the cap. This desk recorded that page on 30 October 2026.

Using FuelEconomy.gov's example of 35 kilowatt-hours per 100 miles, 26.32 pence per kilowatt-hour works out to about £9.21 per 100 miles. That arithmetic is this desk's, not Ofgem's. The standing charge is paid every day whether or not the car is plugged in, so it is left out of the per-mile figure. A car that uses more or less energy per mile changes the result in proportion.

The [Energy Saving Trust's charging guide](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/charging-electric-vehicles/), last updated on 23 April 2026, gives average costs for 220 miles of charge: £17 charging at home, £37 at public fast chargers, £53 at public rapid chargers, against £45 for a full tank of petrol. Those figures predate the October to December 2026 cap. The trust says a home chargepoint needs a driveway or garage and is the cheapest way to charge, and that the installer usually registers the device with the Distribution Network Operator. It points to a UK Government grant of up to £350 per socket for residential landlords, people living in flats and people who rent. It says EV and other time-of-use tariffs, including off-peak tariffs such as Economy 7, can cut the cost of charging overnight, and that these typically need a smart meter.

## How fast each level charges

The price per kilowatt-hour does not say how long a charge takes. The [Alternative Fuels Data Center charging-stations page](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-stations) says charging time can range from less than 20 minutes on a DC fast charger to 20 hours or more on Level 1. It says the time depends on how depleted the battery is, how much energy it holds, the battery type, the vehicle's onboard charger, and the equipment, including the charging level, the power output, and the electrical service. This desk recorded that page on 27 October 2026. It does not show a publication day.

The page's headings give typical added range. AC Level 1, through a 120-volt plug, is about 5 miles of range per hour of charging. A footnote says that figure assumes 1.9 kilowatts. The page says 8 hours at 120 volts can replenish about 40 miles for a mid-size electric vehicle. AC Level 2, through 240 volts in a typical home or 208 volts in a typical commercial building, is about 25 miles of range per hour. It says most residential Level 2 chargers operate at up to 30 amps, delivering 7.2 kilowatts, and need a dedicated 40-amp circuit under National Electric Code Article 625. A footnote says a Level 2 unit can range from 2.9 to 19.2 kilowatts. DC fast charging, which the page also calls Level 3, is about 100 to 200 or more miles of range per 30 minutes, at stations with power outputs up to 500 kilowatts. A footnote says a DC unit can provide up to 500 kilowatts, and that charging power varies by vehicle and by the battery's state of charge. The Energy Saving Trust's rule of thumb is that a charger's power in kilowatts roughly equals the miles of range added in 20 minutes, so a 7 kW home unit adds roughly 7 miles every 20 minutes. Its table, which assumes the battery starts at 20 percent, puts a 50 kWh battery at 7 hours 45 minutes on a 7.4 kW AC charger and 53 minutes on a 50 kW DC charger, and it says rapid charging can slow above 80 percent.

As of 2023, the same page says, less than 1 percent of public charging ports in the United States were Level 1, nearly 80 percent were Level 2, and more than 20 percent were DC fast chargers. Those are shares of public ports. They are not a count of chargers in garages. The page names three DC fast connector types: the SAE Combined Charging System, CHAdeMO, and J3400. It says the Megawatt Charging System is under development for medium- and heavy-duty vehicles, at up to 3.75 megawatts for short stops and below 500 kilowatts for overnight charging. That system is not home Level 1 or Level 2 equipment. On the same page, it says many electric vehicles on the roads today are not capable of charging above 200 kilowatts, and that most new models will be able to charge at higher rates.

[FuelEconomy.gov's charging table](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/charging.shtml) prints the same three bands: 5 miles of range per hour for Level 1 at 120 volts, 25 miles per hour for Level 2 at 240 or 208 volts, and 100 to 200 or more miles per 30 minutes for fast charging, which the table labels 480 volts or more. It says the Level 2 value is an average that can range from a few miles per hour to around 40. It says fast charging is also called DC fast charging, is usually along heavy traffic corridors, is not practical for home installation, and that not all vehicles can accept it. A note says the charge rate also depends on the vehicle, the battery's state of charge, and the ambient temperature. This desk recorded that page on 27 October 2026. It does not show a publication day. Range loss in a cold laboratory test is a different measurement, covered in [cold weather and electric-car range](/articles/2026-10-25-ev-range-cold-weather/).

On the charger itself, the [home-charging page](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-charging-home) says state and utility incentives may be available to help offset the cost of charging equipment. FuelEconomy.gov says incentives may offset some of the cost of a home unit. Neither page prints a national total for those incentives, and this explainer does not list state programs. A posted peak from a car company is also not these typical rates. [BMW's U.S. release on an iX5 at up to 460 kilowatts](/articles/2026-10-26-bmw-ix5-460kw-charging/) and [Chery's stated 1.44 megawatt charger](/articles/2026-10-24-chery-volt-dragon-charging/) are separate claims on this desk. [BYD's third-generation Qin Plus DM-i](/articles/2026-10-27-byd-qin-plus-dm-i-third-gen/) includes a reported charge from 30 percent to 80 percent in as little as 20 minutes, and the outlet does not name the power. The data center's "less than 20 minutes" line is its general range for DC fast charging. It is not that car's specification.

## Analysis

Three official pages answer three different questions. EIA's 18.16 cents is the August 2026 U.S. residential average revenue per kilowatt-hour, released 23 October 2026, and EIA calls it a proxy. FuelEconomy.gov's $45.50 a month uses $0.13 per kilowatt-hour, 35 kilowatt-hours per 100 miles, and 12,000 miles a year, all at home. The data center's about $0.03 a mile and about $6 for a full charge use 10.7 cents and its 27 kilowatt-hour or 54 kilowatt-hour examples. The state averages that update names run from 8.91 cents in North Dakota to 29.02 cents in California, and it limits the high end to the contiguous United States. The $400 to $1,000 line is FuelEconomy.gov's range for a home charger unit. The electricity, the charger, and a public session are three bills. In Great Britain, Ofgem's 26.32 pence is a capped average for a default tariff paid by Direct Debit from 1 October to 31 December 2026, not a price for a fixed or EV tariff, and the Energy Saving Trust's £17 for 220 miles at home was published before that cap began.

Those bills are separate from charging speed. The data center's typical bands are about 5 miles of range per hour at Level 1, about 25 at Level 2, and about 100 to 200 or more miles in 30 minutes on DC fast equipment it rates at up to 500 kilowatts. It says power still varies by vehicle and by state of charge, and that many vehicles on the road today cannot charge above 200 kilowatts. A company peak is not that typical band. The incentive lines say help may be available. They do not name an amount.
