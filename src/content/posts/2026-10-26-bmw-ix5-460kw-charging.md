BMW Group, IONNA, and Alpitronic said they demonstrated high-power charging for the new BMW iX5 60 xDrive at an international media event in Southern California. In the [U.S. press release](https://www.press.bmwgroup.com/usa/article/detail/T0462001EN_US/bmw-group-ionna-and-alpitronic-join-forces-to-demonstrate-high-power-charging:-up-to-460-kw-for-the-new-bmw-ix5-60-xdrive?language=en_US) dated 20 October 2026, BMW says faster charging for that model means up to 460 kW of peak DC power, up to 182 miles added in 10 minutes, and a 144 kWh net pack that goes from 10 to 80 percent in 22 minutes. The 182-mile figure is labeled EPA. A footnote marks both that figure and the 22-minute figure as estimates based on BMW AG testing using EPA test procedures. The same release says the 460 kW experience is a technology demonstration and does not represent charging hardware commercially available in the United States today.

The [global English release](https://www.press.bmwgroup.com/global/article/detail/T0461971EN/bmw-group-ionna-and-alpitronic-join-forces-to-demonstrate-high-power-charging:-up-to-460-kw-for-the-new-bmw-ix5-60-xdrive?language=en), also dated 20 October and datelined Laguna Hills, uses the same peak and the same 22 minutes from 10 to 80 percent. It does not match the U.S. page on the other two numbers. It says the net pack is 141 kWh, and that 10 minutes can add up to 226 miles, which it also prints as 363 km, on the WLTP cycle. It does not call those figures EPA estimates. It repeats that the 460 kW experience is a technology demonstration and is not charging hardware commercially available in the U.S. today. This desk opened both pages on 26 October. The gap is between two BMW releases, not between BMW and an outside measurement.

Both releases call the iX5 the first battery-electric BMW X5 and say it is being launched with the sixth generation of BMW eDrive, after that generation debuted in the iX3. They describe Gen6 as 800-volt technology with longer range, faster charging, and bidirectional charging, plus new high-voltage batteries that use cylindrical cells. The U.S. text says those cells deliver 20 percent greater energy density than the Gen5 prismatic cells. The global text says 20 percent increased volumetric energy density. The words are not the same, and neither page prints a cell capacity in watt-hours that would let a reader reconcile them. Bidirectional charging is listed as a feature of the generation. The releases describe the Southern California event as a high-power charging demonstration, not as a public test of power flowing back out of the vehicle.

The U.S. release is datelined Woodcliff Lake, New Jersey. The global release is datelined Laguna Hills. Both quote Dr. Thomas Engelhardt, senior vice president for high-voltage battery and charging, on showing "the full high-power charging potential of the new BMW iX5" to global media in Southern California. That is BMW's account. This desk was not there and has no separate meter reading.

IONNA is the network side of the event in both releases. Seth Cutler, its chief executive, is quoted saying the collaboration is "translating next-generation charging technology into an experience drivers can trust." Alpitronic is described as supplying the high-power hardware, up to 460 kW, with BMW and IONNA. Philipp Senoner, its chief executive and co-founder, is quoted saying vehicle innovation and charging infrastructure "must advance together." Those are the companies' descriptions of their roles. The U.S. release still says the hardware is not commercially available in the United States today.

BMW's [U.S. consumer page](https://www.bmwusa.com/vehicles/x-series/suv/bmw-ix5.html) for the iX5, which showed no publication date when this desk recorded it on 26 October 2026, prints two charging times. One block says that with a maximum of 460 kW, a driver can charge from 10 to 80 percent in about 23 minutes at a high-power charger, and that the time assumes the vehicle charges at its maximum rate. A later answer says that at a public DC fast charger the iX5 charges from 10 to 80 percent in as fast as about 22 minutes at 460 kW. The press releases this desk opened both say 22 minutes, not about 23. The consumer page also shows an estimated range of up to 435 miles and says ranges are preliminary BMW AG estimations, that they may not yet be fully certified by the EPA, and that the EPA publishes a blended average across wheel and tire combinations that may differ. That 435-mile line is a vehicle-range claim on a product page. It is not the 182 miles the U.S. release says can be added in 10 minutes, and it is not the 226 miles on the global release.

The global release also prints a combined electric power consumption, according to WLTP, of 23.9 to 20.1 kWh per 100 km. The U.S. release this desk opened does not print that range. It does not print a full certified EPA range for the iX5 either. The footnote on the U.S. charging estimates points to BMW AG testing using EPA procedures, which is the same kind of caveat the consumer page uses for its preliminary range.

## Analysis

The figure a reader can treat as shared, because both press releases print it, is a stated peak of up to 460 kW and a stated 10-to-80 percent time of 22 minutes, on hardware BMW says is not commercially available in the United States. The 144 kWh and 182-mile EPA lines belong to the U.S. release, with the miles and the 22 minutes marked as BMW estimates. The 141 kWh and 226-mile WLTP lines belong to the global release. The "about 23 minutes" line belongs to the U.S. product page, which also says "about 22 minutes" further down. None of those pages is an independent lab report, and none says a public network in the U.S. currently offers 460 kW to this model. A 10-minute range and a full-charge range are different claims, and an EPA-procedure estimate is not the same document as a WLTP figure. A public fast-charge demonstration is also a different bill from home electricity, which is the subject of [what it costs to charge an electric car at home](/articles/2026-10-26-ev-charging-cost/).
